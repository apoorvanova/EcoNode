# EcoNode — building the schematic from a completely blank sheet, network by network

Starting assumption: empty KiCad project, nothing placed yet. We build in 8 networks. Before any of that, one mechanism you need to understand, because you'll use it constantly:

## Why we use "net labels" instead of drawing physical wires everywhere

In KiCad, two pins are electrically connected if EITHER (a) a drawn wire physically joins them, OR (b) they both have a text label with the exact same name (e.g. two pins each labeled `GND`) — even with no visible wire between them at all. We use labels for anything that needs to reach far across the sheet (like GND, which touches nearly every part) because drawing a literal wire from every ground pin to every other ground pin would turn the schematic into an unreadable spider web. Labels let the schematic stay clean while still being 100% electrically correct. This is not a shortcut or a simplification — professional schematics are built this way.

**Critical detail:** the label text must match EXACTLY — same capitalization, no extra spaces. `GND` and `Gnd` are two different, disconnected nets to KiCad, even though a human reading them would assume they're the same thing. This single detail causes more first-schematic bugs than almost anything else.

## Network 1 — SOLAR_IN

**What it is:** the raw, unstable voltage straight off the solar panel — roughly 5V in good light, dropping toward 0V in shade. This net is intentionally short-lived; it only exists for the few centimeters between the panel and the charging chip, because nothing else in the circuit should ever see this raw, unpredictable voltage directly.

**Wiring:**
1. Solar cell's `+` terminal → place a net label reading `SOLAR_IN`.
2. TP4056's `VCC` pin → place a net label reading `SOLAR_IN` (identical text, so they merge into one net).

**Why VCC specifically, and why nowhere else:** the TP4056 is the *only* device designed to accept this unstable input — its whole internal job (Stage 2 of the theory) is to take this wobbly, weather-dependent voltage and turn it into a controlled charging current for the battery. Every other chip in this design (the regulator, the MCU, the sensor) is built to expect a clean, known voltage — feeding them raw solar directly would expose them to voltage swings and possible overvoltage they're not rated for.

## Network 2 — VBAT

**What it is:** the raw battery voltage, swinging between ~3.0V (empty) and ~4.2V (full) depending on charge state. Covered in depth earlier — this is the "messy, real-world" voltage that hasn't been cleaned up yet.

**Wiring:**
1. TP4056's `BAT` pin → net label `VBAT`.
2. Battery's `+` terminal → net label `VBAT`.
3. TPS63020's `VIN` pin → net label `VBAT`.
4. TPS63020's `VINA` pin → net label `VBAT`.

**Why BAT and the battery's `+` share this net:** the TP4056's `BAT` pin is literally its charging output — it's the pin that pushes charging current *into* the battery and also the pin the battery's stored voltage is read back *from*. They have to be the same electrical point, because that's the same physical wire in a real board.

**Why VIN and VINA both connect here, not just VIN:** `VIN` is the regulator's main power input — the thing being converted. `VINA` is a separate, dedicated supply pin for the chip's own internal control/reference circuitry (the "brain" that decides how to switch, as opposed to the "muscle" that does the actual switching). Both pins need battery voltage to function, but keeping them as electrically separate *pins on the chip* (even though they're the same net electrically) lets the manufacturer isolate the sensitive internal reference from noise generated by the higher-current switching happening at VIN — that's why VINA gets its own smaller decoupling cap in a moment, rather than sharing VIN's caps directly.

**Decoupling capacitors on this net — and why two 10µF in parallel instead of one 20µF:**
1. Place two 10µF capacitors between the `VBAT` net and `GND`, positioned near TP4056/TPS63020's VIN pin.
2. Place one 1µF capacitor between `VBAT` (well, technically the VINA connection point) and `GND`, near VINA specifically.

**Why capacitors here at all:** the TPS63020 is a *switching* regulator — internally, it's rapidly connecting and disconnecting VIN to the inductor many times per second. Each time it does this, it briefly demands a pulse of current. If that pulse had to travel all the way back to the battery every single time, the wire's own resistance and inductance would cause the voltage to sag and ring with every switch cycle. A capacitor sitting right at VIN acts as a local, instantly-available charge reservoir — it supplies these rapid pulses locally so the battery only has to supply the *average* current, not the sharp instantaneous peaks.

**Why TWO 10µF in parallel, specifically, rather than one 20µF cap:** this is a genuine, deliberate engineering choice, not an arbitrary duplication — two reasons:
- **Ripple current splitting:** every real capacitor has a maximum ripple current rating (how much rapidly-alternating current it can handle before overheating due to its own internal resistance, called ESR — equivalent series resistance). When you put two identical capacitors in parallel, the switching current splits roughly evenly between them. Each capacitor experiences only about half the ripple current it would if it were doing the job alone — meaning less self-heating, and a longer, safer operating life for each part.
- **Lower combined ESR:** when capacitors are in parallel, their ESR values also combine in parallel (like resistors), meaning the *combined* ESR is lower than either capacitor alone. Lower ESR means the capacitor bank responds faster and holds the voltage steadier during those switching pulses — directly improving regulator stability.

So "two in parallel" isn't redundancy for its own sake — it's genuinely a better electrical solution than one bigger part, and it's exactly what you'll see in real manufacturer reference designs.

**Why VINA's cap is smaller (1µF) and separate from VIN's caps:** VINA only feeds the chip's internal low-current reference/control circuitry, not the high-current switching path — it doesn't need the large charge reservoir that VIN's switching action demands. A smaller cap here is sufficient and also physically isolates VINA's quiet supply from VIN's noisier one, reinforcing the separation the chip manufacturer intended.

## Network 3 — GND

**What it is:** one single shared ground reference for the entire board — not owned by any one device, as clarified earlier.

**Wiring — every one of these pins gets a net label reading exactly `GND`:**
1. Solar cell `−` terminal
2. TP4056 `GND` pin, and `EPAD` (its thermal pad)
3. Battery `−` terminal
4. Both 10µF VIN capacitors — the leg NOT connected to VBAT
5. The 1µF VINA capacitor — the leg NOT connected to VBAT
6. TPS63020 `GND` pin, `PGND` pin, and `PAD` (thermal pad)
7. TPS63020 `PS/SYNC` pin (explained below)
8. Both 10µF VOUT capacitors — the leg NOT connected to 3V3 (covered in Network 4)
9. The 180kΩ feedback resistor — the leg NOT connected to FB (covered in Network 5)
10. ESP32-C3 `GND` pin
11. The 0.1µF ESP32 decoupling cap — the leg NOT connected to 3V3
12. BME280 both `GND` pins
13. The 0.1µF BME280 decoupling cap — the leg NOT connected to 3V3
14. LED cathode (the non-pointy end of the diode symbol's triangle)

**Why PS/SYNC goes to GND specifically:** this pin selects between two operating modes on the TPS63020 — tying it to GND selects "power-save mode," where the chip automatically reduces its own switching activity (and therefore its own power consumption) when the load current is very low. Since your ESP32 spends 99%+ of its time in deep sleep drawing only microamps, power-save mode means the regulator itself also wastes less energy during that same idle time — this directly supports the whole "31-day battery life" goal from the theory calculation. Tying it the other way (to VOUT) would force constant fast switching regardless of load, which is simpler for some noise-sensitive applications but wastes power exactly when you have almost no load — the wrong tradeoff for a battery-powered device.

**Why the thermal pads (EPAD, PAD) need GND too:** these aren't electrical signal pins in the traditional sense — they're a exposed metal pad on the underside of the chip package used to conduct heat away into the PCB's copper. By convention, they're tied to ground both because ground is usually the largest, most thermally-connected copper area on a board, and because leaving them floating can cause the chip's internal ground reference to be electrically unstable.

## Network 4 — 3V3

**What it is:** the clean, stable, regulated output — the whole reason the TPS63020 exists. This is what every downstream chip actually consumes.

**Wiring:**
1. TPS63020 `VOUT` pin → net label `3V3`.
2. Two 10µF capacitors between `3V3` and `GND`, placed right at the VOUT pin.
3. ESP32-C3 `3V3` pin (pin 1) → net label `3V3`.
4. A 0.1µF capacitor between `3V3` and `GND`, placed right at the ESP32's 3V3 pin.
5. BME280 `VDD` pin → net label `3V3`.
6. BME280 `VDDIO` pin → net label `3V3`.
7. BME280 `CSB` pin → net label `3V3`.
8. A 0.1µF capacitor between `3V3` and `GND`, placed right at the BME280's VDD pin.

**Why the VOUT capacitors matter more than any other capacitor in the whole design:** this is the single most important decoupling point on the board. The TPS63020's entire feedback control loop (Network 5) is watching *this exact node* to decide how hard to switch. If this node is noisy or unstable, the regulator's own control loop can become unstable too — in the worst case, oscillating or "hunting" for the right output level rather than settling on a steady 3.3V. The datasheet's specified output capacitor value isn't a suggestion, it's a stability requirement for the feedback loop itself.

**Why the ESP32's own 0.1µF cap is still needed even though VOUT already has 20µF of capacitance:** distance matters more than total capacitance here. The VOUT capacitors are electrically "far" from the ESP32 in terms of PCB trace length — even a short trace has some resistance and inductance, which slows down how quickly charge from those capacitors can reach the ESP32 during a sudden demand. When the ESP32 wakes from deep sleep, its current demand jumps from ~20µA to ~120mA in a fraction of a microsecond — faster than charge can travel any meaningful distance along a trace. A capacitor sitting immediately at the ESP32's own power pin can respond at that same instant, because there's essentially no trace length for the electrical response to lag behind. This is why "put a cap right at the pin" is a rule regardless of how much capacitance already exists elsewhere on the same net.

**Why CSB connects to 3V3, not to a GPIO or left floating:** this is a mode-select pin, not a power pin in the traditional sense — tying it permanently high tells the BME280's internal logic "operate in I2C mode," as opposed to its alternate SPI mode. It needs to be in a fixed, known state at all times (not toggled, not floating), which is exactly what tying it to a stable power rail achieves.

## Network 5 — FB (feedback)

**What it is:** a small, internal-feeling net that exists purely to tell the TPS63020 what voltage it's currently producing, so it can correct itself in real time. This is NOT a power-carrying net — almost no current flows through it.

**Wiring:**
1. TPS63020 `FB` pin → net label `FB`.
2. One leg of the 1MΩ resistor → net label `FB`. Other leg → net label `3V3`.
3. One leg of the 180kΩ resistor → net label `FB`. Other leg → net label `GND`.

**Why this needs two resistors forming a divider, rather than wiring FB straight to VOUT:** the TPS63020's internal reference only recognizes a small fixed voltage (0.5V for this chip) as "correct." If you wired FB directly to the 3.3V output, the chip would see 3.3V on its feedback pin and — thinking that's way too high compared to its internal 0.5V reference — would aggressively reduce switching, trying to pull its own output down, and you'd end up with a much lower, wrong output voltage. The resistor divider's job is to scale the real 3.3V output down to exactly 0.5V at the FB pin, so the chip's internal comparison actually works correctly.

**Why 1MΩ specifically goes on the "top" (between 3V3 and FB), and 180kΩ on the "bottom" (between FB and GND) — not the other way around:** the formula governing this divider is:

`Vout = 0.5V × (1 + R_top / R_bottom)`

Plugging in R_top = 1MΩ and R_bottom = 180kΩ: `0.5 × (1 + 1,000,000/180,000) = 0.5 × (1 + 5.56) = 0.5 × 6.56 ≈ 3.28V` — very close to the target 3.3V (small differences are normal and fine; this is why you cross-check against the datasheet's own table rather than trusting hand math alone). If you swapped the resistors — putting 180kΩ on top and 1MΩ on the bottom — the math would instead compute a *much lower* target voltage, well under 1V, which is wrong. The direction genuinely matters, it's not a symmetrical choice.

**Why the values are specifically ~1MΩ and ~180kΩ, and not some other pair that also satisfies the ratio (e.g. 100kΩ and 18kΩ, same ratio):** larger resistor values mean less current constantly flows through the divider itself (current = voltage / resistance, so higher resistance = less wasted current). Since this divider is active 100% of the time the regulator is on, using large resistor values here keeps this "always-on background current" tiny, protecting your battery-life number. The datasheet's own recommended values already balance this against a competing concern (values too large make the feedback node more sensitive to electrical noise) — which is exactly why you use the datasheet's specific recommended pair rather than picking arbitrary values that merely satisfy the ratio.

## Network 6 — SDA

**What it is:** the data line of the I2C bus — the wire that literally carries sensor readings from the BME280 to the ESP32.

**Wiring:**
1. ESP32-C3 `IO08` pin → net label `SDA`.
2. BME280 `SDI` pin → net label `SDA`.

**Why GPIO8 specifically, and why it's called `SDI` on the BME280 side even though it's functionally SDA:** covered in an earlier correction — GPIO8 is a genuinely free pin on the ESP32-C3 with no conflicts (unlike GPIO21/22, which don't apply to this chip, or GPIO20/21 which are UART). The BME280 physically supports both I2C and SPI protocols on the same physical pins, and its datasheet labels this specific pin with its SPI name (`SDI`) by default — once you tie `CSB` high (Network 4), the chip's internal logic reinterprets this same physical pin as I2C's `SDA` function. The pin doesn't move, its *meaning* changes based on the CSB setting.

---

## Network 7 — SCL

**What it is:** the clock line of the I2C bus — a steady timing signal the ESP32 generates so the BME280 knows exactly when to read each bit of data on the SDA line.

**Wiring:**
1. ESP32-C3 `IO09` pin → net label `SCL`.
2. BME280 `SCK` pin → net label `SCL`.

**Why a separate clock line is needed at all (not just relying on SDA alone):** I2C is a synchronous protocol — both devices need to agree on the exact instant to read/write each bit. Without a shared clock, the ESP32 and BME280 would have no way to stay in sync about when one bit ends and the next begins. The clock is generated solely by the ESP32 (the "master") and the BME280 (the "slave") simply follows it.

**Why SDO needs its own connection too (technically part of setting up this same bus, worth including here):** BME280 `SDO` pin → net label `GND`. This isn't SDA or SCL, but it's part of I2C setup: SDO's wiring state sets the chip's I2C address — tying it to GND selects address `0x76`, tying it to 3V3 instead would select `0x77`. This has to match whatever address your firmware calls in `bme.begin(0x76)`.

## Network 8 — LED_CTRL

**What it is:** a dedicated, firmware-controlled signal — deliberately kept separate from the always-on 3V3 rail.

**Wiring:**
1. ESP32-C3 a spare GPIO (e.g. `IO16`) → net label `LED_CTRL`.
2. One leg of the 330Ω resistor → net label `LED_CTRL`. Other leg → LED anode.
3. LED cathode → net label `GND` (already covered in Network 3).

**Why this is wired to a GPIO instead of directly to 3V3:** wiring the LED straight to the always-on 3.3V rail would mean it's permanently lit, drawing current 24/7 — and as calculated earlier, even a modest ~4mA constant draw would exceed your entire sleep-phase power budget, wrecking the 31-day battery life estimate for the sake of a status light. Wiring it to a GPIO instead means firmware decides exactly when it's on — e.g., flash it for a fraction of a second during each wake cycle — keeping its total energy cost negligible.

**Why the resistor value is 330Ω, worked from scratch:** the governing formula is `R = (V_supply − V_LED_forward) / I_desired`. With a 3.3V supply, a typical LED forward voltage of roughly 2.0V, and a deliberately modest target current of about 4mA (bright enough to see, but power-conscious): `(3.3 − 2.0) / 0.004 = 325Ω` — rounded up to the nearest standard resistor value, 330Ω. Using a lower resistance would push more current through the LED (brighter, but burns more power and risks exceeding the LED's maximum rated current); a much higher resistance would make it dimmer but sip even less power — 330Ω is a reasonable, commonly-used middle ground.

## Final step — run ERC and read every single warning individually

**Inspect menu → Electrical Rules Checker → Run.** For every warning that appears, resolve it deliberately rather than dismissing it:
- **"Pin not connected"** on a pin you're genuinely not using (like TP4056's CHRG/STDBY status outputs) → click the pin, press **Q** to add an explicit no-connect flag.
- **"Pin not connected"** on a pin that's supposed to be wired → you've found a real gap; go back to the relevant network section above and check you actually placed the label.
- **Net label mismatch** (two pins that look like they should connect, but ERC treats them as separate nets) → check for a typo or capitalization difference between the two label instances — this is the single most common beginner error with label-based wiring, mentioned at the very top of this guide.

Once ERC shows zero unresolved warnings, this schematic is electrically complete and internally consistent — the next real checkpoint after that is comparing your finished TPS63020 section side-by-side against its datasheet's typical application circuit, to catch anything this guide's reasoning might have simplified.
